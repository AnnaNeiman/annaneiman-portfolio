The 60-second version

The concept, the screens, and the system that outlasted us.

  1. 1

    The problem

    Most customers never found the streaming apps in their TV plan. We had 2.5 weeks, and midway the business decided no one would be staffed to maintain the page.

  2. 2

    The move

    Build on the homepage's row of cards (a swimlane) that had just shipped, cut the card to what it needed, and make every image, label, and limit safe for someone who isn't a designer to update.

    Upgrades up 37%. Six providers added after launch with no design interventions.

01 · Context

The problem was never the first screen. It was every screen after launch.

Spectrum TV plans included streaming apps like ESPN, Peacock, and Paramount+, and most customers never found them. Competitors had polished marketplaces. We had the partnerships but no single place to show them.

Leadership approved a personalized storefront concept for a CEO shareholder unveil. We asked for 10 weeks and got 2.5. The concept pictured one customer type and needed about four bespoke card layouts plus a CMS anyone could use.

Midway, the business decided nobody would be staffed to update the page's art. After launch, a small product team with no designer or engineer behind it took ownership. The job changed from designing the concept to designing a system that anyone could operate safely.

03 · Key decisions

Six decisions, and what each one gave up.

Decision 1

Built on the homepage swimlane that had just shipped

Link
✗Rejected
Why it lost

Too much to build in 2.5 weeks.

Engineering faced about four custom card layouts and a CMS anyone could use.

✓Shipped
Why it won

It shipped, and it was accessible.

The swimlane container and card had already passed accessibility review and were cheap to extend.

The trade-off: the concept's range of card layouts gave way to two cards and an existing banner. Reuse bought speed and gave up some of the concept. A long-title stress test (“Super Long TV Show Name: Season XYZ”) went into the dev specs, so engineering knew where the card breaks.

↻

Where we changed direction

We matched the approved card, found it broke, and simplified it twice.

The approved card carried dates, buttons, titles, branding, and promos. It broke on long titles and on mobile.

So we cut further than felt comfortable until the show art became the hero.

Decision 2

Chose “Activate” over the exec-favored “Unlock”

Link
✗Rejected
Why it lost

Read as an extra payment.

Customers took “Unlock” as an extra payment, and it was hard to translate into Spanish.

✓Shipped
Why it won

Says exactly what to do.

“Activate” translates cleanly and reads clearly with a screen reader.

The trade-off: we went against what execs wanted, so the case had to hold up. My content-design partner led the language call: she showed execs that “Unlock” read as a charge and failed in Spanish and with screen readers. I built the component system that carries “Activate” and the full state vocabulary across every card. The word later went into the guidelines the page owner works from.

Decision 3

Gave the hero one 16:9 image, a safe area, and a gradient

Link
✗Rejected
Why it lost

Unreadable on busy art.

Text sat directly on rotating images, and it failed accessibility.

✓Shipped
Why it won

Readable on any image.

The business would not staff hero art, and providers could not be asked for new art every week. One 16:9 image with a gradient over the bottom third keeps text readable. The safe area is the part of the image that survives every crop, so titles and key art stay inside it.

The result: I designed the gradient and the image spec. My content-design partner defined the safe-zone rules: character limits, headline-to-CTA order, and per-segment targeting. The exec got the look, marketing got autonomy, customers got readable text, and accessibility approved it.

Decision 4

Designed the states the concept skipped, including a customer with no TV plan

Link
✗Rejected
Why it lost

It pictured one customer.

Customers without TV, with mixed apps, or with nothing left to activate had no screen.

✓Shipped
Why it won

One card, 10+ states.

Every customer sees a page that fits their plan.

One card, five of its 10+ states. The customer's state picks the variant.
Customer state Card says

The trade-off: custom cards launch faster, but each one needs its own contrast and focus fixes, and they drift apart. One shared card took more work up front and almost none per new provider. A senior leader wanted TV plans to get more emphasis, which led to a state for customers without TV who can add apps a la carte and choose a TV plan. The page runs Ready to Watch, Activate, Upgrade & Add, Most Popular, then New Seasons, so customers see what they can watch before they're asked to buy.

Decision 5

Wrote the handoff for an owner with no designer

Link
  • Fixed containers: image ratio, safe area, row height, and copy limits are set in the templates, so most mistakes stay harmless.
  • A copy deck in two languages, embedded in Figma, so engineering and marketing work from the same words.
  • Logic in Jira for each customer state.
  • A guidelines file for the page owner, written by my content-design partner and me: component and image specs from me, voice and template standards from her.

The trade-off: fixed containers limit what marketing can try. They also keep the page working when the person updating it isn't a designer.

Decision 6

Coded the logo sizing rule with a dev lead so logos size themselves

Link
  • The problem: provider logos arrive in many shapes, and the owner team has no designer to size each one by hand.
  • The rule: two classes by aspect ratio, wide and tall. A logo falls into one class and the container sizes it.
  • How it got built: I worked out the rule and coded the logo containers with a dev lead, so the design and the build matched.

The trade-off: two classes give up a perfect fit for every mark. They give every logo a predictable fit, and a new logo needs no design time.

04 · Build & validate

A usability test found what the prototype got wrong.

UX research tested a working prototype with 8 customers on August 5 and 6, 2025. It is a small sample, so we read it as directional. UX research wrote it up to shape the fast-follows planned alongside launch.

The prototype still said “Unlock Included Streaming Apps.” The shipped vocabulary is Activate, with the state set around it. Pre-launch testing also found the state language clear and the simplified cards easier to take in.

  • Tasks: describe the page, find items, then buy, upgrade, and watch.
  • Finding: 3 of 8 failed the Watch task. The “Ready to Watch” section title implied instant playback, and the Watch Now button sat low on the app detail page.

Where customers got stuck: the app detail page.

06 · After launch

The system kept changing shape as the constraints changed.

  • October 2025: the public launch was October 9. A dynamic hero carousel followed on October 17.
  • November 2025: the product team asked for title attribution on every card. The image provider only supplies standard 16:9 and 3:4 ratios with attribution built into the image. So the cards moved to standard ratios with no gradient. The hero kept its gradient. We also added play and pause to the carousel.
  • Accessibility review: on 10/06/2025 the review asked for 3.5:1 contrast on the carousel arrows, which is hard over busy images, and for the carousel position to be announced to screen readers. Both were resolved in the carousel delivered 10/17.
  • Logos: sized by the two-class rule in Decision 6.

Each answer replaced the last one, and each one kept the same idea: a fixed container and flexible content.

08 · Reflection

I'd ask who runs the page on day one, and bring research in first.

  • Ask who will operate the page after launch, before designing anything.
  • Bring research in on day one.
  • Test on mobile earlier.
  • Phase the launch: core swimlanes first, hero carousel and targeting after.
  • Test CTAs by segment, something my content-design partner and I would build in from the start.

09 · How it was built

Built on Kite, specced for three audiences.

We treated the handoff as part of the design.

Tools
Figma, on Kite (Spectrum's design system). Drupal for the CMS, so marketing can update the page without a code change.
Engineering
I specced every state: spacing, type, and color tokens, focus states, ARIA notes, 44px touch targets, breakpoint guides, and a long-title stress test.
Marketing
My content-design partner wrote the content templates with character limits, CMS guides for hero targeting, voice and tone guidelines, and state-language rules.
Page owner
Together we wrote the Marketplace Guidelines: image and component specs, plus voice and template standards.
Change
Engineering built from the specs without waiting on design, and new providers launched with no design help.
