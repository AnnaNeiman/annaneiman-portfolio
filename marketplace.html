The concept, the screens, and the system that outlasted us.

01 · Context

Ten weeks asked, 2.5 given, then no one was staffed to run the page.

Spectrum TV plans included streaming apps like ESPN, Peacock, and Paramount+, and most customers never found them. Competitors had polished marketplaces. We had the partnerships but no single place to show them.

Leadership approved a personalized storefront concept for the CEO's July 2025 shareholder update. My content design partner, our design lead, and I asked for 10 weeks. Leadership needed it in about 2.5, from about 6/10 to the 6/27 dev handoff. That date was the forcing function, so the first question on every decision was what already existed.

The concept pictured one customer type. By my count it needed about four bespoke card layouts plus a CMS anyone could use.

About halfway through, the business decided a product team with no designer or engineer behind it would own the page, and that no one would be staffed to make art for it. No custom imagery, no weekly asks of providers, no designer in the loop. The job changed from designing the concept to designing a system that anyone could operate safely.

02 · Key decisions

Six decisions, and what each one gave up.

Decision 1

Built on the homepage swimlane that had just shipped

Link
✗Rejected
Why it lost

Too much to build in 2.5 weeks.

Engineering faced about four custom card layouts and a CMS anyone could use.

✓Shipped
Why it won

It already existed.

The container had scroll, arrows, fade, and snap. The card had states, hover, and focus. Marketplace became a new variant of an existing card.

The trade-off: the concept's range of card shapes gave way to two cards and an existing banner, so marketing got fewer layouts. I started from what engineering already had and asked what could ship in the window, and we rebased the card and swimlane on it. Our read was that customers choose by the show they want to watch, so the art leads the card. Upgrade and Add share one swimlane, because the technical team said two would be more complex, and the MVP has no shopping cart.

↻

Where we changed direction

We matched the approved card, found it broke on a long title, and simplified twice.

A “Super Long TV Show Name: Season XYZ” broke the approved layout, and the card broke again on mobile. We reworked the alignment and hierarchy first. It still carried too much, and we spent a day on a direction that didn't pan out.

So we cut further than felt comfortable, down to the title, the date, and the app logo, until the show art became the hero. The long title went into the dev spec as a stress test, so engineering knew where the card breaks.

Decision 2

Chose “Activate” over the exec-favored “Unlock”

Link
✗Rejected
Why it lost

Abstract, and it failed two checks.

“Unlock” was abstract, it did not translate into Spanish, and it failed accessibility.

✓Shipped
Why it won

Says exactly what to do.

“Activate” names the action, and it holds up in Spanish and with a screen reader.

The trade-off: we went against what execs wanted, so the case had to hold up. My content design partner led the language case with the execs, and I led with the design: a component system that carries “Activate” and the full state vocabulary across every card. Together we held the line. The word later went into the guidelines the page owner works from. UX research's August 2025 usability study, run after the 6/27 handoff, found TV customers read “Unlock” as an extra payment. It confirmed a call we had already made.

Decision 3

Designed the states the concept skipped, including a customer with no TV plan

Link
✗Rejected
Why it lost

It pictured one customer.

Customers without TV, with mixed apps, or with nothing left to activate had no screen.

✓Shipped
Why it won

One card, 10+ states.

Every customer sees a page that fits their plan.

One card, five of its 10+ states. The customer's state picks the variant.

The trade-off: more states to specify up front. A senior leader wanted more TV plan sales, so we asked what a customer with no TV plan would want to see. The answer is a page that offers apps to add, or a TV plan to choose with the apps that come with it. My content design partner's state vocabulary and my variants are one system: each state is both a word and a component variant. A new provider fits an existing state, so it needs no new card. The page runs Ready to Watch, Activate, Upgrade & Add, Most Popular, then New Seasons, so customers see what they can watch before they're asked to buy.

Decision 4

Gave the hero one 16:9 image, a safe area, and a gradient

Link
✗Rejected
Why it lost

Unreadable on busy art.

Text sat directly on rotating images, and it failed accessibility.

✓Shipped
Why it won

Readable on any image.

The business would not staff hero art, and providers could not be asked for new art every week. One 16:9 source image serves every placement, and each container crops from it. A gradient over the bottom third keeps text readable even on a weak image. The safe area is the part of the image that survives every crop.

The result: I designed the gradient and the image spec. My content design partner set the safe-zone content rules: character limits, headline-to-CTA order, and which copy appears for which customer segment. Neither works without the other. The exec got the look, marketing got autonomy, customers got readable text, and accessibility approved it. What it gave up: a month after launch the gradient left the cards too little safe area, and the cards dropped it (see After launch).

Decision 5

Put every variable input in a fixed container, for an owner with no designer

Link
  • Fixed containers: images by ratio and safe area, copy by character limit, the hero by four call-to-action states, providers by content key. The limits sit inside the templates, so most mistakes stay harmless.
  • A copy deck in two languages, embedded in Figma, so engineering and marketing work from the same words.
  • Logic in Jira for each customer state, and static copy kept apart from dynamic copy.
  • A guidelines file for the page owner, written by my content design partner and me: voice and template standards from her, component and image specs from me.
  • A declined option: a hero call to action derived from what each customer owns. A stakeholder wanted it. We kept one image and one CTA, chosen in the CMS from four recommended states (Activate, Watch, Upgrade, Add), which cut build cost.

One card, fixed zones. The art leads, and the logo, title, and state label each get a set space and a character limit.

The trade-off: fixed containers limit what marketing can try, and the hero has less personalization at launch. They also keep the page working when the person updating it isn't a designer.

Decision 6

Coded the logo sizing rule with a dev lead so logos size themselves

Link
  • The problem: provider logos arrive in many shapes, and the owner team has no designer to size each one by hand.
  • The rule: two classes by aspect ratio, wide and tall, each with its own flexbox. A logo falls into one class and the container sizes it. The logo and the CTA text share one fixed-height row, so the text stays on one line.
  • How it got built: I worked out the rule and the flexbox logic one to one with a dev lead, so the design and the build matched. It came in the November card update, after launch.

The trade-off: two classes give up a perfect fit for every mark, and ratios between the classes are not covered yet. Every logo gets a predictable fit, and a new logo needs no design time.

04 · After launch

Each new constraint replaced the last answer.

  • October 2025: the public launch was October 9, with stand-in imagery in places because good assets weren't available. A dynamic hero carousel followed on October 17.
  • Accessibility review: the review asked for more contrast on the carousel arrows, which is hard over busy images, and for the carousel position to be announced to screen readers. Both were resolved in the October 17 carousel.
  • November 2025: about a month after launch, the page owner team asked for title attribution on every card. Providers place titles differently in their art, and the gradient left too small a safe area. The image provider supplies standard 16:9 and 3:4 ratios with attribution built in, so the cards moved to those ratios with no gradient. The hero kept its gradient. We also added play and pause to the carousel. Accessibility approved the package on 11/06/2025.
  • Logos: sized by the two-class rule in Decision 6.

Each answer replaced the last one, and each round also cut build and maintenance cost.

06 · Reflection

The research came after handoff. Next time it comes first.

  • Ask who will operate the page after launch, before designing anything.
  • Bring research into the room on day one. UX research tested a working prototype on August 5 and 6, 2025, after handoff and before launch. It showed that “Ready to Watch” set an expectation of instant playback while the Watch Now button sat low on the app detail page. We would have wanted that before handoff.
  • Test on mobile earlier.
  • Phase the launch: core swimlanes first, hero carousel and targeting after.
  • Test CTAs by segment, something my content design partner and I would build in from the start.
  • Post-launch tracking points at the next problem: awareness kept rising while confidence in understanding the offer fell, and unclear activation steps stayed the top barrier.

07 · How it was built

Built on Kite, specced for three audiences.

We treated the handoff as part of the design.

Tools
Figma, on Kite (Spectrum's design system). Drupal for the CMS, so marketing can update the page without a code change.
Engineering
I specced every state: spacing, type, and color tokens, focus states, ARIA notes, breakpoint guides, and the long-title stress test.
Marketing
My content design partner wrote the content templates with character limits, CMS guides for hero targeting, voice and tone guidelines, and state-language rules.
Page owner
Together we wrote the Marketplace Guidelines: image and component specs, plus voice and template standards.
